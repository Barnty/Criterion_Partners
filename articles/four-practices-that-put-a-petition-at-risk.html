Four practices that put an EB-1A petition at risk

Paid journals, traded citations, bought awards and placed press. Why they are being scrutinised, and how to stay clear.

As interest in EB-1A has grown, so has a market selling shortcuts. News reports and immigration attorneys have described closer scrutiny of petitions built on purchased credentials, including approved cases being looked at again.

USCIS has said publicly that people who submit fake evidence or misrepresent themselves will face consequences.

Four practices come up again and again.

1. Pay-to-publish journals

Real peer review takes weeks or months. A journal that accepts a paper within days of payment is not reviewing it. Officers can check a journal's reputation, indexing and editorial board in minutes.

Do instead: submit work that is genuinely yours to journals with a real editorial process, and accept that rejection is part of it.

2. Traded citations

Groups of authors who agree to cite each other leave an obvious pattern: the same names, the same small set of journals, sudden jumps in numbers.

Do instead: make your work easy to find and use. Preprints, repositories, conference talks. Citations that come from people who needed your work hold up.

3. Awards you can buy

If the only barrier to an award is the entry fee, it says nothing about excellence. The criterion asks for nationally or internationally recognised prizes for excellence.

Do instead: target awards with a published selection process and a record of past winners.

4. Placed press

A press release you or a consultant wrote, published on a site that prints anything for a fee, is not "published material about you" in major media. It is an advert.

Do instead: earn coverage. A journalist choosing to write about your work is the evidence.

The simple test

Before adding anything to your profile, ask: if an officer investigated this for ten minutes, would it look stronger or weaker? Build only what looks stronger.

This article is general information, not legal advice. It summarises public reporting and our own practice standards. Speak to a licensed immigration attorney about your own case.
