Judging the work of others is one of the ten EB-1A criteria, and for most researchers peer review is the most direct way to meet it. The good news is that journals need reviewers far more than most people realise. The hard part is getting on an editor's radar, and then doing the work in a way that holds up as evidence.

## Why editors need you

Every submitted manuscript needs two or three reviewers, and editors often contact many people before enough agree. Someone who replies quickly, reviews carefully and delivers on time is valuable. Once an editor trusts you, invitations tend to follow.

## How to get on an editor's radar

1. **Publish first, even modestly.** Editors usually find reviewers through the reference lists and author databases of papers in their own field. Having your name on relevant published work is the most common route in.
2. **Register with publisher reviewer systems.** Many publishers let you create a profile and list your areas of expertise. Make those keywords specific: "federated learning for IoT security" finds you; "computer science" does not.
3. **Keep a public research profile.** An up-to-date ORCID, Google Scholar or institutional page lets an editor check you are real and relevant in seconds.
4. **Tell editors you have worked with.** If you have published in a journal, a short, polite note offering to review in your specialism is normal and often welcome.
5. **Start with conferences.** Programme committees review many submissions in a short window and often take on new reviewers each cycle.

## Do the review well

A review an editor can act on is what earns the next invitation.

- Summarise the paper in two or three sentences so the editor knows you understood it.
- Separate major concerns from minor ones.
- Be specific: point to the section, figure or equation.
- Be fair and professional, even when you recommend rejection.
- Deliver by the deadline. Late reviews are the most common reason editors stop asking.

## Document it as you go

For EB-1A, an invitation alone is weak evidence. What counts is proof that you **completed** the review. Keep:

- Certificates or letters from the journal confirming completed reviews
- Thank-you messages from editors that name the manuscript
- Your reviewer record from the publisher's system

Do not include invitations that were later withdrawn. They show you were asked, not that you judged anything.

## Quality over volume

An officer weighs where you reviewed, not just how often. A handful of completed reviews for established, indexed journals from recognised publishers carries more weight than a long list from outlets nobody in your field reads.
