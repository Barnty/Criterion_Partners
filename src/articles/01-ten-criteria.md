The EB-1A category is for people with extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business or athletics. USCIS says you must show **sustained national or international acclaim**. There are two ways to do that: one major, internationally recognised award (think Pulitzer, Oscar or Olympic medal), or evidence under at least three of the ten criteria below.

Most people take the second route. Here is what each criterion means in everyday language.

## The ten criteria

1. **Awards.** Nationally or internationally recognised prizes for excellence in your field. A prize anyone can buy with an entry fee does not count for much.
2. **Membership.** Membership in associations that demand outstanding achievement of their members. Paying a subscription is not achievement. Being elected or vetted by experts is.
3. **Published material about you.** Articles about you and your work in professional publications, major trade publications or other major media. Written by someone else, about you.
4. **Judging.** Evidence that you have been asked to judge the work of others, alone or on a panel. Peer review for a journal is the classic example.
5. **Original contributions.** Original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic or business contributions of major significance to the field. The words "major significance" do the heavy lifting here.
6. **Scholarly articles.** Your authorship of scholarly articles in professional or major trade publications or other major media.
7. **Exhibitions.** Your work displayed at artistic exhibitions or showcases.
8. **Leading or critical role.** A leading or critical role in distinguished organisations.
9. **High salary.** A high salary or other significantly high pay compared with others in your field.
10. **Commercial success.** Commercial successes in the performing arts.

If a criterion does not readily apply to your occupation, USCIS allows **comparable evidence** instead.

## Three criteria is the entry ticket, not the prize

This is the part most applicants miss. Meeting three criteria makes you eligible to be considered. It does not make you approved.

After counting the criteria, the officer looks at everything together and asks a bigger question: does this record show sustained acclaim, and that this person is among the small percentage at the very top of the field? This second look is often called the final merits determination.

That is why a thin tick in three boxes loses to a strong, connected record in three boxes.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting quantity over quality.** Ten papers in journals that accept anything are weaker than two in journals with real peer review.
- **Evidence nobody can verify.** If an officer cannot confirm an award or a publication independently, it carries little weight.
- **Shortcuts.** Paid journals, traded citations and bought awards can sink a petition, and reports say some approvals have been revisited because of them.

## What to do next

List what you already have under each criterion. Be honest about which items would survive a close read. That list is the start of a real plan.
