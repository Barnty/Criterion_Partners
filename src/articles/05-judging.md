One of the ten EB-1A criteria is evidence that you have been asked to judge the work of others, either individually or on a panel. For researchers, this is often the most reachable criterion. It is also one of the easiest to get wrong.

## What qualifies

- **Peer review for journals.** Reviewing manuscripts submitted to scholarly journals.
- **Conference review.** Serving on a programme committee or reviewing submissions.
- **Editorial roles.** Serving on an editorial board.
- **Panels.** Judging grant applications, competitions or awards in your field.
- **Academic judging.** Serving on a thesis or dissertation committee.

## Being invited is not enough

The evidence should show you **actually did the judging**, not only that someone asked. An invitation email on its own is weak. A request that was later withdrawn is not evidence of judging at all.

Strong evidence looks like this:

- A certificate or letter from the journal confirming completed reviews
- A thank-you message from the editor naming the manuscript you reviewed
- A reviewer record from the publisher's system

## Where you review matters

Officers weigh the quality of the venue. A completed review for an established, indexed journal from a recognised publisher says more than twenty reviews for an outlet nobody has heard of.

Ask of any venue: does it have a real editorial board, real rejection rates and a publisher people in your field respect?

## How to get started

1. Create reviewer profiles with publishers in your field and list your expertise accurately.
2. Tell editors you have published with that you are available to review.
3. Do each review carefully and on time. Editors invite good reviewers back.
4. Save every confirmation and certificate as you go.

## How it fits the bigger picture

Judging on its own will not carry a petition. It works best alongside your own publications and evidence that others use your work. Together they show a person the field trusts.
